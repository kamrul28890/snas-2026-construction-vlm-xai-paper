\begin{abstract}

Construction safety depends on small, rule-specific visual details: a hard hat on a particular worker, a barrier at a particular edge. Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed to check such details automatically, but they can give the right safety answer for the wrong visual reason, judging a scene unsafe because construction sites look hazardous while ignoring the cue the rule concerns. Existing evaluations report answer accuracy and are silent on this failure. We introduce ConstructionSafety-FaithBench, an audit benchmark separating three properties usually merged into one score: whether the answer is correct, whether the cited visual evidence is rule-relevant, and whether the answer changes when that evidence is removed. Evidence dependence is probed by occluding the rule-relevant region and comparing the effect against size-matched occlusions placed elsewhere, so any difference reflects location rather than the amount removed. The benchmark is model-agnostic; we validate it on one open-vocabulary grounding pipeline built on Florence-2, across hard-hat, fall-protection, guardrail, and struck-by rules, and for that system the three properties come apart. It reaches 18.3\% accuracy on 120 adjudicated hard cases and returns an evidence region on 98.8\% of images, yet those regions overlap the rule-relevant one poorly, mean intersection-over-union 0.058. It nonetheless flips its answer 39.2\% of the time under targeted occlusion against 9.2\% under matched controls, a paired difference of 30.0 points, and never answers ``uncertain.'' Coming from one system on one imagery source, these findings characterize that pipeline rather than VLMs in general, but they indicate that answer accuracy is insufficient evidence of trustworthiness for safety-critical screening, and that a system's willingness to abstain deserves scrutiny alongside its accuracy. Benchmark, code, and frozen artifacts: \url{https://github.com/kamrul28890/snas-2026-construction-safety-faithbench}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Construction and extraction occupations accounted for 1,032 fatal work injuries in the United States in 2024, 370 of them falls, slips, and trips \citep{bls2026cfoi}, and regulators group the leading causes of construction death into falls, struck-by incidents, caught-in or caught-between events, and electrocution \citep{osha_focusfour}. Most such hazards are visible in the moments before they cause harm, so safety supervision is largely a visual task limited by coverage, and sites already produce ample imagery that could be checked automatically against written rules. Vision-language models (VLMs), which take an image with a natural-language prompt and return a natural-language response, are an appealing tool for this, and some also return the image regions they associate with that response. But safety-critical use changes what counts as an acceptable answer: a missed fall-protection violation can precede a fatal fall, and a confidently reported hazard that does not exist erodes the trust that makes the tool usable. It is not enough to be right on average; a reviewer needs to know whether a verdict rests on the visual evidence the rule actually concerns.

Existing evaluation does not close this gap. Benchmarks for construction-safety VLMs report answer accuracy, which is silent on whether the answer used the right evidence \citep{chen2025inspectors}, and broader work on rationale quality is not specific to safety rules or their localized, object-level cues \citep{li2025rationales}. Most directly, MERLIM identifies \textit{hidden hallucinations}, correct answers produced without meaningful visual grounding, reporting that instruction-tuned models often succeed by exploiting global scene patterns and language priors \citep{villa2025merlim}; it establishes the phenomenon on general imagery but does not test whether it persists when a written rule designates which region is decisive, which is the situation a safety inspection creates. Methodologically, explanation-faithfulness research has shown that internal scores such as attention weights can look convincing while failing to track what a model uses \citep{jain2019attention,wu2024faithfulness}, motivating tests of behavior under controlled intervention and aligning with the requirements set out for explainable systems \citep{phillips2021principles}.

This paper separates three properties that a single accuracy score merges. They come apart in practice: a system can be correct without being grounded, by inferring ``unsafe'' from generic construction-scene clutter, and it can return a plausible-looking region without that region driving its answer. We ask:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (Correctness).} How accurately does the evaluated grounding pipeline answer construction-safety rule questions, scored against adjudicated reference labels?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (Evidence relevance).} Does the visual evidence the pipeline returns correspond to the region the safety rule actually concerns?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (Intervention sensitivity).} Does removing the rule-relevant region change the pipeline's answer more than removing an equally sized region elsewhere in the same image?
\end{itemize}

None of the three establishes \textit{faithfulness}, the stronger property that an answer genuinely depends on the evidence that ought to support it. Intervention sensitivity is necessary but not sufficient: a changed answer shows dependence on the occluded location, yet a black rectangle also introduces an artifact the system may react to, and an unchanged answer may reflect redundant cues or language priors rather than an absent dependence. We therefore report sensitivity as an observable behavior and treat faithfulness as an interpretive question this evidence informs without resolving.

To answer them we introduce ConstructionSafety-FaithBench, an audit benchmark pairing each construction-site image with a written safety rule, an answer label, the evidence regions the rule depends on, and label provenance, across four rule families matched to the leading hazard categories: harness and fall protection and guardrail and edge protection, both addressing falls; struck-by hazards from equipment proximity; and hard-hat and personal protective equipment (PPE) compliance. The schema, metrics, and protocol are model-agnostic, and any system returning an answer with evidence regions can be run through them; the empirical results come from a single system, so findings are phrased throughout as properties of the evaluated pipeline rather than of the model class. The study audits an existing system rather than proposing one, and describes measurement, not deployment readiness.

\section{Benchmark and Audit Pipeline}

\textbf{Images and safety rules.} The benchmark is built from ConstructionSite imagery, released with safety-oriented captioning, rule-violation, and object-grounding annotations \citep{chen2025inspectors} and chosen because generic object-detection corpora do not carry safety semantics. Each row pairs one image with one written rule and records the source split, the safety question, a normalized rule identifier, the answer label, the rule-relevant evidence regions as \textit{bounding boxes} in pixel coordinates, and label provenance. Answers are \textit{compliant}, \textit{violation}, or \textit{uncertain}, the last meaning the image cannot support a decision; that third label matters because a system forced to choose between the first two will guess when it should defer. A 163-row pilot split supports the intervention study and a 588-row scale-up split tests consistency at larger scale. Images and model weights are not redistributed.

\textbf{The evaluated pipeline.} The system under audit locates candidate objects, then applies a fixed rule to those locations. Locating uses the \textit{microsoft/Florence-2-base-ft} release of Florence-2, which accepts an image with a text prompt and returns bounding boxes for the objects that prompt describes \citep{xiao2024florence}, a capability called \textit{open-vocabulary grounding}: the model is not restricted to trained categories, so it can be asked for ``hard hat'' or ``guardrail'' in words, without retraining per rule. It is small enough to run repeatedly on modest hardware, which the intervention study requires. Around it a \textit{grounding adapter} applies fixed geometric tests to the returned boxes, checking for a hard-hat rule whether a helmet box overlaps each detected worker's head region. These tests are \textit{deterministic}, with no learned parameters, so any failure traces to the grounding step or the rule logic rather than hiding inside a second black box. Three blind reference points bound the problem from below: \textit{manifest-seed} echoes the stored label, \textit{majority-violation} always answers ``violation,'' and \textit{caption-keyword} reads only the caption.

\textbf{From image to score.} Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the full path from image and rule to the three scores. Every stage writes normalized CSV and JSONL records, so each reported number traces back to the run that produced it.

\textbf{The audit label layer.} Reference labels are built in three layers, and their quality bounds what the evaluation can claim. Automated baseline annotations from the dataset's own metadata scale cheaply but derive from the same metadata used to assemble the splits, so they are never treated as independent ground truth. A prioritized audit set of 120 rows is drawn by conditioning on pipeline--baseline disagreement, where expensive adjudication is most informative; 118 of the 120 are outright answer disagreements. \textbf{The audit set is therefore enriched for disagreement and is not a representative sample}, so its statistics estimate performance on contested cases and every audit-set figure below is reported alongside the corresponding full-split figure. Each audited row then receives two independent passes, with the 12 disagreements resolved by a returned adjudication step. The passes agree on 108 of 120 rows, but raw agreement flatters a set in which one label dominates, so we report Cohen's $\kappa = 0.78$ against an expected agreement of 0.54. That is substantial consistency in applying the protocol, not evidence that the protocol is correct, because both passes were generated by AI annotators working from the same written instructions rather than by independent human domain experts.

We therefore call the result \textit{reference labels} rather than ground truth. The task mixes perceptual judgments a careful non-expert can answer reliably, such as whether a helmet is visible on a given head, with safety judgments that depend on training and site experience, such as whether a given proximity to operating equipment is unsafe; only the first is well supported by our procedure. The six rows adjudicated \textit{uncertain} concentrate in exactly the judgment-dependent cases, such as a static frame that cannot establish an excavator's swing radius. That these labels have not been validated by independent construction-safety professionals is the single largest threat to the validity of every accuracy figure we report.

\section{Metrics and Experimental Setup}

Each research question requires a different measurement, and the central design decision of this benchmark is to keep them apart.

\textbf{Answer metrics (RQ1).} \textit{Accuracy} is the proportion of rows where the pipeline's answer matches the audit label. It is misleading under class imbalance: because most audited rows are violations, a system that always answers ``violation'' scores well without understanding anything. \textit{Macro-F1}, the equally weighted average of the per-class F1 scores for compliant, violation, and uncertain, corrects for this, so a model handling only the majority class scores high on accuracy and low on macro-F1. Both are reported as percentages and higher is better; the gap between them is diagnostic of a system exploiting the label distribution rather than reading the image.

\textbf{Evidence metrics (RQ2).} \textit{Evidence presence} records whether the pipeline returned any region at all, which matters because a system that silently returns nothing cannot be audited and an unexplained verdict is not actionable. \textit{Box overlap} compares the returned region with the rule's reference region using intersection over union (IoU), the area two rectangles share divided by the area they jointly cover, where 1.0 means they coincide and 0 that they do not touch. \textit{Normalized centroid drift} measures how far the center of the returned region moves as a fraction of the image diagonal, so the number is comparable across image sizes. We report drift alongside IoU because the two disagree systematically on small objects: for a small hard hat a modest shift destroys IoU while barely moving the centroid, so IoU alone would overstate the instability.

\label{sec:intervention}
\textbf{Intervention metrics (RQ3).} Faithfulness cannot be read off a single run, because a correct answer and a well-placed box are both compatible with the model ignoring the evidence entirely, so we intervene on the image and observe what changes. For each image a \textit{targeted occlusion} places a solid black rectangle over the first rule-relevant object box in the manifest: the head region for hard-hat rows, the exposed edge for guardrails, the worker-at-height region for fall protection, and the worker--equipment proximity region for struck-by risk. Each matched-random control uses identical width and height at a seeded random location chosen to keep target overlap at or below 0.05 IoU, falling back to the lowest-overlap candidate rather than discarding the image. Five controls per image give 948 runs: 158 targeted and 790 control.

Any occlusion changes the image, so a change in answer proves nothing on its own; the control is what makes this a test rather than an observation. Because the number of occluded pixels is held constant, a systematic difference between conditions cannot be attributed to the amount removed. The size match holds exactly: mask area is identical by construction in both conditions, mean share 0.136 and median 0.014 of the image, while mask--target IoU is 1.000 for targeted masks against 0.056 for matched-random ones, so the conditions differ in placement rather than extent.

The control isolates less than it may appear. It establishes that the effect is not produced by removing pixels as such, since 88.2\% of random masks overlap the target at an IoU of 0.05 or below while occluding an identical area, but not that the rule-relevant region is special among \textit{informative} regions: a random mask disturbs content the pipeline was using only 1.4\% of the time against 5.1\% for targeted masks, so the comparison separates rule-relevant evidence from arbitrary image area rather than from other meaningful content. A near-target control is the most valuable single extension to the protocol. Two further caveats, detailed with the supporting statistics in the released analysis scripts: the control degenerates on the 13 of 158 targets covering more than half the image, where an equally sized mask cannot avoid the target box, so we repeat the primary comparison without them; and a black rectangle is not a natural occlusion, which size matching equalizes but does not eliminate.

The primary statistic is the \textit{answer-flip rate}, the proportion of images whose answer changes after occlusion, reported as a paired difference defined per image as the targeted outcome minus the mean of that image's five controls, so that pairing removes differences in scene difficulty. Intervals come from image-level bootstrap resampling with 10{,}000 resamples, and paired comparisons use the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test with rank-biserial effect size and Holm correction. The full configuration, covering model and dataset revisions, decoding settings, mask construction, placement search, and seeds, is frozen in \texttt{analysis/protocol.json} in the released repository.

\textbf{Answer correctness (RQ1).} The key comparison is between the automated baseline and the grounding pipeline on the same 120 adjudicated rows, of which 83 are violations, 31 compliant, and 6 uncertain. The automated baseline reaches 78.3\% accuracy, but this number should not be read as competence. That baseline is derived from the same dataset metadata used to assemble the candidate split, so it is partly scoring itself. Its macro-F1 of 50.5\% exposes the difference: it handles the majority violation class and fails elsewhere. For reference, the two audit passes themselves score 93.3\% and 95.8\% against the final adjudicated labels.

Florence-2 reaches 18.3\% accuracy and 12.5\% macro-F1 here. Two caveats keep this from being a general performance claim: the audit set was built from disagreement and weak-overlap cases, and on the full splits scored against the weaker metadata-assisted labels the same pipeline reaches 41.7\% on the pilot and 39.3\% on the scale-up; and the score describes the complete pipeline, grounding plus rule logic, not Florence-2's detection ability in isolation.

Accuracy is the wrong summary for a safety task, because it prices a missed hazard and a false alarm identically. The third block of Table~\ref{tab:headline} separates them: the pipeline reported \textit{compliant} for 70 of 83 genuine violations, a missed-violation rate of 84.3\%, while raising false alarms on 22 of 31 compliant rows, against 13.3\% missed for the metadata-assisted baseline. A false alarm costs an unnecessary inspection; a missed violation is the failure the system exists to prevent.

Two patterns matter more than the headline number. The pipeline never returned \textit{uncertain} on any of the 120 rows, despite six being adjudicated undecidable from a single frame, and on the hard-hat and guardrail slices it answered \textit{compliant} for all 30 rows in each, though 26 and 24 respectively were violations. A system that cannot abstain and cannot flag a violation in an entire rule family fails silently, in the direction that matters most for safety.

Three blind baselines set the floor: manifest-seed scores 100\% by construction, confirming that portion of the annotation is circular; majority-violation reaches 69.3\% on the pilot and 57.5\% on the scale-up split; and caption-keyword reaches 19.7\%, so the labels are not simply recoverable from captions. Answering ``violation'' every time therefore outperforms the grounding pipeline, and accuracy alone cannot demonstrate that a model is reading the image.

\textbf{Evidence quality (RQ2).} Correctness says nothing about whether the pipeline pointed at the right thing. It almost always produces evidence, returning at least one region for 96.9\% of pilot and 98.8\% of scale-up rows, but those regions agree poorly with the rule's reference region: mean best IoU is 0.369 on the pilot and only 0.058 on the scale-up split, meaning the returned and rule-relevant rectangles barely intersect.

The rule-level breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:rulelevel} explains the gap between splits and is the more informative result. Hard-hat rows dominate the scale-up split, 303 of 588, with the worst overlap at 0.030, and struck-by rows are similar at 0.033. These are exactly the families whose evidence is small or relational: a hard hat occupies a few dozen pixels on a worker's head, and equipment proximity is a relationship between two objects rather than one object with a boundary. Guardrail and fall-protection evidence is physically larger and overlaps better, at 0.171 and 0.130.

The consequence is specific: a reviewer would receive a bounding box on essentially every image, which looks like a system that always shows its work, yet on hard-hat rows that box would usually not contain the hard hat in question. Evidence presence is not evidence quality, and an interface displaying model evidence without scoring it creates confidence the underlying measurement does not support.

\textbf{Evidence dependence (RQ3).} The comparison to read in Figure~\ref{fig:intervention} is targeted against size-matched random occlusion, since both remove identical numbers of pixels. Occluding the rule-relevant region changed the answer 39.2\% of the time against 9.2\% for matched controls: a paired difference of 30.0 percentage points, bootstrap 95\% interval 22.3 to 37.7, rank-biserial effect size 0.89. Evidence relocation shows the same pattern, with a paired centroid-drift difference of 0.166, interval 0.140 to 0.193, Holm-adjusted $p < .001$. Excluding the 13 images where the control degenerates, the difference grows slightly to 31.7 points across the remaining 145; we report the pooled figure as the more conservative of the two.

This cuts both ways. The model is not merely reacting to generic construction-scene clutter: were it doing so, the paired difference would sit near zero, and it does not. But sensitivity is not sound reasoning. When rule-relevant evidence disappears, the appropriate response for a safety system is to report that it can no longer tell; instead the pipeline substitutes a different confident verdict, consistent with the complete absence of \textit{uncertain} answers under RQ1. In a deployed screening tool, an occluded, dirty, or badly angled view of a worker would not produce a flag for human review but a different answer, delivered with the same confidence as the original.

The three results are therefore not redundant: the pipeline is often wrong (RQ1), usually points somewhere unhelpful (RQ2), and is nonetheless measurably sensitive to the right pixels (RQ3). Any one measured alone would mislead.

\section{Discussion and Implications}

Correctness, grounding, and evidence dependence dissociate: a benchmark reporting only the first would call this system weak, one reporting only the third would call it evidence-driven, and the safety-relevant behavior sits in the disagreement. A plausible explanation is the difference between detecting an object and adjudicating a rule. Open-vocabulary grounding answers ``where is X,'' whereas a safety rule asks whether every worker satisfies a requirement, a quantified judgment that often turns on \textit{absence}: no hard hat on that head, no barrier at that edge. A detector that fails to find an object cannot distinguish that failure from absence, so the rule logic adopts a default verdict for ungrounded cases, and that default then answers every case where grounding fails, consistent with the all-compliant behavior on the hard-hat and guardrail slices, precisely the families with the weakest overlap.

The implication concerns failure mode rather than accuracy. A screening tool that misses hazards is dangerous; one that misses them while displaying a confident verdict and a plausible box is worse, because it supplies the visual trappings of justification. What matters is not only how often a system is right but whether it knows when it cannot tell. Two recommendations follow: report an abstention rate alongside accuracy, treating a system that never returns ``uncertain'' as unvalidated for review support; and score displayed evidence regions rather than merely rendering them, since unscored evidence invites reviewers to assume a grounding that has not been demonstrated.

These findings extend to a safety-critical visual domain the established results that plausible-looking explanations need not reflect what a model uses \citep{jain2019attention,wu2024faithfulness} and that correct answers can be produced without visual grounding \citep{villa2025merlim}. Our addition is the rule-defined reference region: a safety rule specifies in advance which region should be decisive, which makes the targeted intervention well posed. They also qualify optimism about general-purpose VLMs for construction inspection \citep{chen2025inspectors}, since the difficulty is not detection but the translation from detections into rule verdicts, where safety semantics live.

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}

\textbf{Model and annotation.} One system was evaluated, so every empirical statement here describes that pipeline rather than compact grounding models generally, and comparative claims need a wider suite. Reference labels come from two AI passes plus adjudication, not from independent construction-safety professionals; the safety-judgment portion of that task is least supported by the procedure and determines most reported accuracies.

\textbf{Selection and scale.} The audit set conditions on pipeline--baseline disagreement, so its statistics describe contested cases; full-split figures accompany every audit-set figure, but the enrichment cannot be undone analytically. Coverage is 163 pilot and 588 scale-up rows from one collection and is sharply imbalanced: scanning 3,004 source rows yielded only 13 struck-by-risk and 75 fall-hazard candidates against 250 PPE-violation and 250 compliant, so those two families are diagnostic rather than stable estimates. Site types, regions, weather, and equipment conventions are not systematically varied, four rule families stand in for safety programs encompassing many more, and every judgment is made from a single frame, leaving hazards defined by motion or duration genuinely undecidable.

\textbf{Intervention and interpretation.} As Section~\ref{sec:intervention} sets out, a black rectangle is not a natural occlusion, the controls separate rule-relevant evidence from arbitrary image area rather than from other informative content, and they degenerate on the 13 largest targets. Sensitivity is an observable behavior, not a demonstration of faithful reasoning: a changed answer shows dependence on the occluded location and an unchanged one is equally ambiguous. Evidence is also compared only at the level of rectangles, which cannot capture whether the model used the correct part of a correctly located object.

\textbf{Scope.} The benchmark does not measure human-like or causal reasoning, internal chain-of-thought, general interpretability, deployment reliability, injury reduction, or how reviewers respond to displayed evidence. It measures three observable behaviors on a fixed set of images and rules and supports comparison under a shared protocol; it is one mechanism within a trustworthy-AI evaluation programme, not such a programme on its own.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

A camera installed to check hard-hat compliance can equally support productivity monitoring, biometric identification, or retrospective review of a worker's conduct, and footage gathered for one purpose is routinely available for another. Safety monitoring does not justify persistent worker surveillance; the two should be separated through retention limits, restrictions on secondary use, meaningful consent, and worker representation in deployment decisions. The benchmark infers no identity, intent, competence, or blame, and should not be used for autonomous inspection or disciplinary monitoring: because the failure modes documented here fall hardest on missed violations, deploying such a system without human review would shift risk onto workers while presenting an appearance of oversight. Appropriate use is research evaluation under documented scope limits, data minimization, source-license compliance, and human professional review, consistent with risk-management guidance for AI-enabled workplace tools \citep{nist2023airmf,howard2024workplace}.

\section{Conclusion}

ConstructionSafety-FaithBench separates answer correctness, evidence grounding, and evidence dependence rather than collapsing them into one score. Auditing an open-vocabulary grounding pipeline across four construction rule families, we found the three come apart: 18.3\% accuracy on an adjudicated set of hard cases, an evidence region returned on 98.8\% of images but overlapping the rule-relevant region with mean IoU of 0.058, and answers that changed 39.2\% of the time under targeted occlusion against 9.2\% under size-matched controls. It never once answered ``uncertain.''

Answer accuracy is therefore insufficient evidence of trustworthiness in safety-critical visual applications, and faithfulness claims require interventions with size-matched controls rather than inspection of plausible-looking explanations. A screening tool's willingness to abstain deserves as much scrutiny as its accuracy, because the dangerous failure is not an obvious error but a confident verdict accompanied by evidence no one has scored. Replacing the AI-pass audit layer with domain-expert annotation, extending the model suite to stronger instruction-following VLMs, and testing how human reviewers respond to displayed evidence are the natural next steps.
