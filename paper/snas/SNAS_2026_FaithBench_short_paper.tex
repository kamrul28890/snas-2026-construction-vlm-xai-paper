\begin{abstract}

Construction safety depends on small, rule-specific visual details: a hard hat on a particular worker, a barrier at a particular edge. Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed to check such details automatically, but they can give the right safety answer for the wrong visual reason, judging a scene unsafe because construction sites look hazardous while ignoring the cue the rule concerns. Existing evaluations report answer accuracy and are silent on this failure. We introduce ConstructionSafety-FaithBench, an audit benchmark that separates three properties usually merged into one score: whether the answer is correct, whether the cited visual evidence is rule-relevant, and whether the answer changes when that evidence is removed. Evidence dependence is probed by occluding the rule-relevant region and comparing the effect against occlusions of identical size placed elsewhere in the image, so any difference reflects location rather than the amount removed. The benchmark is model-agnostic; here we validate it on one system, an open-vocabulary grounding pipeline built on Florence-2, across hard-hat, fall-protection, guardrail, and struck-by rules. For that system the three properties come apart. The pipeline reaches 18.3\% accuracy on 120 adjudicated hard cases. It returns an evidence region on 98.8\% of images, yet those regions overlap the rule-relevant one poorly, mean intersection-over-union 0.058. It nonetheless flips its answer 39.2\% of the time under targeted occlusion, against 9.2\% under size-matched random occlusion, a paired difference of 30.0 points. It never answers ``uncertain,'' even where a single frame cannot support a decision. Coming from one system on one imagery source, these findings characterize that pipeline rather than vision-language models in general. They nonetheless indicate that answer accuracy is insufficient evidence of trustworthiness for safety-critical screening, and that a system's willingness to abstain deserves scrutiny alongside its accuracy. Benchmark, code, and frozen artifacts: \url{https://github.com/kamrul28890/snas-2026-construction-safety-faithbench}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Construction remains one of the most hazardous industries in which to work. Construction and extraction occupations accounted for 1,032 fatal work injuries in the United States in 2024, of which 370 were falls, slips, and trips \citep{bls2026cfoi}. Regulators group the leading causes of construction death into four categories: falls, struck-by incidents, caught-in or caught-between events, and electrocution \citep{osha_focusfour}. Crucially, most of these hazards are visible in the moments before they cause harm. Safety supervision is therefore largely a visual task: a supervisor looks at a specific worker, a specific edge, or a specific machine, and decides whether a specific written rule is being followed. The limiting factor is coverage. One person cannot watch every worker, every unprotected edge, and every moving machine at once, and site conditions change hour by hour.

This coverage gap motivates automated visual screening. Construction sites already produce large volumes of imagery from fixed cameras, drones, and routine progress documentation. If that imagery could be checked automatically against written safety rules, supervisors could be directed toward the situations that most need human attention.

Vision-language models (VLMs) are an appealing tool for this task. A VLM takes an image together with a natural-language prompt and returns a natural-language response; some VLMs can additionally return the image regions they associate with that response. Because the rule is expressed in text rather than trained into a fixed classifier, one model can in principle be asked about hard hats today and guardrails tomorrow without retraining. That flexibility matters in a domain where safety rules are numerous, written in prose, and revised over time.

Safety-critical use changes what counts as an acceptable answer. In ordinary image captioning, a wrong answer is an inconvenience. In safety screening, a missed fall-protection violation can precede a fatal fall, and a confidently reported hazard that does not exist erodes the trust that makes the tool usable at all. It is therefore not enough for a model to be right on average. A reviewer needs to know whether a particular verdict rests on the visual evidence that the rule actually concerns.

That requirement separates properties which are often treated as one. \textit{Correctness} asks whether the system's verdict matches the reference safety label. \textit{Evidence relevance} asks whether the image region the system points to corresponds to the object the rule is about. \textit{Intervention sensitivity} asks whether the verdict changes when that region is removed from the image. These come apart in practice. A system can be correct without being grounded, by inferring ``unsafe'' from generic construction-scene clutter. It can also return a plausible-looking region without that region driving its answer.

We are deliberately careful about how these relate to \textit{faithfulness}, meaning the stronger property that a system's answer genuinely depends on the evidence that ought to support it. The three measurable properties above contribute evidence toward such an assessment, but none of them establishes it. Intervention sensitivity in particular is necessary but not sufficient: an answer that changes when rule-relevant pixels are removed shows dependence on that location, yet a black rectangle also introduces an artifact the system may react to, and an answer that does \textit{not} change may reflect redundant visual cues or language priors rather than an absent dependence. We therefore report sensitivity as an observable behavior and treat faithfulness as an interpretive question that this evidence informs without resolving.

Existing evaluation does not close this gap. Benchmarks for construction-safety VLMs report answer accuracy against labeled images, which measures correctness but is silent on whether the answer used the right evidence \citep{chen2025inspectors}. Broader multimodal work has begun to evaluate the quality of model-generated rationales, but it is not specific to safety rules and does not target the localized, object-level cues that construction rules depend on \citep{li2025rationales}. Most directly, MERLIM identifies what it calls hidden hallucinations, correct answers produced without meaningful visual grounding, and reports that instruction-tuned vision-language models often succeed by exploiting global scene patterns and language priors rather than the visual content actually in question \citep{villa2025merlim}. That work establishes the phenomenon on general imagery. It does not test whether the same failure occurs when a written rule designates which region is decisive, which is the situation a safety inspection creates.

Methodologically, research on explanation faithfulness has repeatedly shown that internal scores such as attention weights can look convincing while failing to track what a model actually uses \citep{jain2019attention,wu2024faithfulness}. That finding motivates testing behavior under controlled interventions rather than inspecting internal scores, and it aligns with the descriptive-accuracy and robustness requirements set out for explainable systems \citep{phillips2021principles}. What is missing is an evaluation that applies such intervention-based tests to construction-safety rules, on construction imagery, using evidence regions defined by the rule rather than by visual salience.

This paper addresses that gap. We ask three questions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (Correctness).} How accurately does the evaluated grounding pipeline answer construction-safety rule questions, scored against adjudicated reference labels?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (Evidence relevance).} Does the visual evidence the pipeline returns correspond to the region the safety rule actually concerns?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (Intervention sensitivity).} Does removing the rule-relevant region change the pipeline's answer more than removing an equally sized region elsewhere in the same image?
\end{itemize}

To answer them we introduce ConstructionSafety-FaithBench, a compact and reproducible audit benchmark for construction-safety vision-language systems. Each example pairs a construction-site image with a written safety rule, an answer label, the evidence regions the rule depends on, and provenance fields recording where each label came from. The benchmark covers four rule families, chosen for their correspondence to the leading hazard categories above. Two of them address falls, the largest single category: harness and fall protection, and guardrail and edge protection. A third covers struck-by hazards from equipment proximity. The fourth, hard-hat and personal protective equipment (PPE) compliance, covers the principal control against head injury arising from either.

It is worth stating plainly what is general here and what is not. The benchmark schema, the metrics, and the intervention protocol are model-agnostic: any system that accepts an image and a written rule and returns an answer with evidence regions can be run through them. The empirical results, by contrast, come from a single system. Nothing in this paper establishes how other vision-language models behave, and we have taken care throughout to phrase findings as properties of the evaluated pipeline rather than of the model class.

The paper makes four contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item An audit benchmark structure for construction-safety vision-language systems that records images, written rules, answers, rule-relevant evidence regions, and label provenance in one schema.
\item An evaluation protocol that measures correctness, evidence relevance, and intervention sensitivity separately rather than collapsing them into a single accuracy number.
\item An intervention design in which each targeted occlusion of rule-relevant evidence is compared against size-matched occlusions placed elsewhere in the same image, so that measured effects cannot be explained by the number of pixels removed, together with a quantitative characterization of what that control does and does not isolate.
\item A demonstration of the framework on one open-vocabulary grounding pipeline, showing that answer accuracy and evidence dependence give different, and partly contradictory, pictures of that system's reliability.
\end{itemize}

We deliberately frame these as evaluation contributions. The study audits an existing system rather than proposing a new one, and its findings describe measurement, not deployment readiness. The next section describes the benchmark and the audit pipeline used to produce these measurements.

\section{Benchmark and Audit Pipeline}

\subsection{Images and Safety Rules}

The benchmark is built from ConstructionSite imagery and its associated metadata, a construction-site collection released with safety-oriented captioning, rule-violation, and object-grounding annotations \citep{chen2025inspectors}. We selected this source because it provides real construction scenes together with the safety-relevant object annotations that a rule-level audit requires; generic object-detection corpora do not carry safety semantics.

Each benchmark row pairs one image with one written safety rule and records seven fields: an image identifier, the source split, the safety question in natural language, a normalized rule identifier, the answer label, the evidence regions the rule depends on, and provenance metadata describing where the label came from. Evidence regions are stored as \textit{bounding boxes}, rectangles specified by their pixel coordinates that mark where in the image a relevant object appears.

Answers use three labels. \textit{Compliant} means the rule is satisfied, \textit{violation} means it is broken, and \textit{uncertain} means the image does not contain enough information to decide. The third label matters for safety review: a system that is forced to choose between compliant and violation will guess when it should defer.

Two splits are used. A 163-row pilot split supports the intervention study, and a 588-row scale-up split tests whether the same pipeline behaves consistently at larger scale. Raw images and model weights are not redistributed; the released scripts load source images only when the user already has local dataset access.

\subsection{The Evaluated Pipeline}

The system under audit answers a safety question in two steps: it first locates candidate objects in the image, then applies a fixed rule to those locations to produce a verdict.

The locating step uses Florence-2, a compact vision-language model that accepts an image together with a text prompt and can return bounding boxes for the objects that prompt describes \citep{xiao2024florence}. This capability is called \textit{open-vocabulary grounding}: the model is not restricted to a fixed list of trained categories, so it can be asked for ``hard hat'' or ``guardrail'' directly, in words, without being retrained for each new safety rule. We evaluate the \textit{microsoft/Florence-2-base-ft} release. We selected it because it is small enough to run repeatedly on modest hardware, which the intervention study requires, and because its prompt-driven grounding matches the way construction rules are written.

Around this model we build what we call a \textit{grounding adapter}: a thin wrapper that turns the model's raw boxes into a rule verdict. For a given rule the adapter prompts Florence-2 for the objects that rule concerns, then applies fixed geometric tests to the returned boxes. A hard-hat rule, for example, checks whether a helmet box overlaps the head region of each detected worker. These tests are \textit{deterministic}, meaning they involve no learned parameters and no randomness: the same boxes always produce the same verdict. We use deterministic geometry rather than a second learned model so that any failure can be traced to either the grounding step or the rule logic, instead of being hidden inside another black box.

We also run three reference points that require no image understanding at all, included to show what performance is achievable without genuinely reading the picture. A \textit{manifest-seed} baseline simply echoes the label already stored in the manifest, and therefore reveals how much of the annotation is circular. A \textit{majority-violation} baseline always answers ``violation,'' establishing the score obtainable by exploiting class imbalance alone. A \textit{caption-keyword} baseline reads only the text caption, never the pixels, and tests whether the captions leak the answer. Together these bound the problem from below: any pipeline that claims to use visual evidence should be judged against what these blind strategies already achieve.

\subsection{From Image to Score}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the full path, which runs as follows. A construction image and a written safety rule enter the pipeline. The grounding adapter prompts Florence-2, which returns candidate boxes. The deterministic rule logic converts those boxes into an answer and a cited evidence region. That answer is compared against the audit label to measure correctness, and the cited region is compared against the rule's reference region to measure evidence relevance. Separately, the rule-relevant region is occluded and the image is re-run, and the change in the answer is compared against the change produced by occluding an equally sized region elsewhere. That comparison measures intervention sensitivity. Every stage writes normalized CSV and JSONL records, so each reported number can be traced back to the run that produced it.

\subsection{The Audit Label Layer}

Scoring requires reference labels, and the quality of those labels bounds what the evaluation can claim. The benchmark therefore builds them in three layers.

First, automated baseline annotations are derived from the dataset's own metadata, captions, rule-violation records, and available boxes. These scale cheaply and support triage, but because they are generated from the same metadata used to assemble the splits, they are not independent ground truth and are never treated as such.

Second, a prioritized audit set of 120 rows is drawn from the full splits by conditioning on disagreement: rows where the Florence-2 pipeline and the automated baseline give different answers, or where their evidence regions barely overlap. Of the 120 audited rows, 118 are outright answer disagreements. The reason for selecting this way is that adjudication is expensive and disagreements are where adjudication is most informative; rows where both sources already agree would consume the same effort to confirm what is not in dispute.

This selection has a direct consequence that we want to state before reporting any number from it. \textbf{The audit set is enriched for disagreement and is therefore not a representative sample.} Statistics computed on it estimate performance on contested cases, not performance across the benchmark or across construction imagery generally. Wherever we report an audit-set figure we also report the corresponding figure from the full, non-enriched splits, so that the two are never confused. It is useful to keep three sets distinct: the \textit{full splits} of 163 pilot and 588 scale-up rows, the \textit{screening stage} that compares pipeline and baseline answers across them, and the \textit{audit set} of 120 rows that screening selects for adjudication and intervention analysis.

Third, every row in that set receives two independent audit passes, with the 12 disagreements between those passes resolved by a returned adjudication step. The two passes agree on 108 of 120 rows, but raw agreement flatters a set in which one label dominates, so we report Cohen's $\kappa$, which corrects for agreement expected by chance: $\kappa = 0.78$ against an expected agreement of 0.54. That is substantial consistency, and it says the protocol is being applied repeatably. It does not speak to whether the protocol is correct, because both passes were generated by AI annotators working from the same written instructions, not by independent human domain experts.

We call the result \textit{reference labels} rather than ground truth throughout, and the distinction is not merely verbal. The annotation task mixes two kinds of judgment with different validity requirements. Whether a helmet is visible on a given head, or where a barrier sits in the frame, is a largely perceptual question that a careful non-expert can answer reliably. Whether a scene constitutes a fall-protection violation, whether a given proximity to operating equipment is unsafe, or whether a rule applies at all in the depicted context are safety judgments that in practice depend on training and site experience. Our labels cover both kinds, but only the first kind is well supported by the procedure we used.

Two consequences follow. Ambiguity is not evenly distributed: the six rows adjudicated \textit{uncertain} are concentrated in exactly the judgment-dependent cases, such as a static frame that cannot establish an excavator's swing radius, and the 12 rows requiring adjudication were disproportionately of this type. And the labels have not been validated by independent construction-safety professionals, which is the single largest threat to the validity of every accuracy figure we report. Independent review by at least two qualified professionals, blinded to model predictions, with inter-rater agreement reported and a documented adjudication procedure, is required before these labels should be treated as authoritative.

These labels provide the reference against which the evaluated pipeline is assessed. The next section defines the metrics that perform that assessment.

\subsection{Answer Metrics (RQ1)}

\textit{Accuracy} is the proportion of rows where the pipeline's answer matches the audit label. It is easy to read but misleading under class imbalance: because most audited rows are violations, a system that always answers ``violation'' scores well without understanding anything.

\textit{Macro-F1} corrects for this. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall for a single label, and macro-F1 averages the F1 scores of the compliant, violation, and uncertain classes with equal weight regardless of how many rows each contains. A model that handles only the majority class therefore scores high on accuracy but low on macro-F1. Both range from 0 to 1, reported here as percentages, and higher is better. The gap between them is diagnostic: a wide gap indicates a system that is exploiting the label distribution rather than reading the image.

\subsection{Evidence Metrics (RQ2)}

\textit{Evidence presence} records whether the pipeline returned any region at all. This matters because a system that silently returns nothing cannot be audited, and in a safety review an unexplained verdict is not actionable.

\textit{Box overlap} compares the returned region with the rule's reference region using intersection over union (IoU), the area shared by two rectangles divided by the total area they jointly cover. An IoU of 1.0 means the two regions coincide exactly and 0 means they do not touch, so higher is better.

\textit{Normalized centroid drift} measures how far the center of the returned region moves, expressed as a fraction of the image diagonal so that the number is comparable across images of different sizes. A drift of 0 means the evidence did not move at all. We report drift alongside IoU because the two disagree systematically on small objects: for a small hard hat, a modest shift in position destroys IoU while barely moving the centroid, so IoU alone would overstate the instability.

\subsection{Intervention Metrics (RQ3)}

Faithfulness cannot be read off a single run, because a correct answer and a well-placed box are both compatible with the model ignoring the evidence entirely. We therefore intervene on the image and observe what changes.

For each image we construct a \textit{targeted occlusion}: a solid mask covering the region the rule depends on. For a hard-hat row this is the worker and head region, for guardrails the exposed edge or barrier, for fall protection the worker-at-height region, and for struck-by risk the worker and equipment proximity region. If the model is genuinely using that evidence, removing it should change the verdict or at least push it toward uncertainty.

Both mask types are solid black rectangles. The targeted mask covers the first rule-relevant object box recorded in the manifest. Each matched-random control uses the identical clipped width and height, placed at a seeded random location: the generator draws up to 200 candidate positions seeking a target-box IoU at or below 0.05, and if none qualifies it keeps the lowest-overlap candidate and reports the resulting overlap rather than discarding the image. Five controls are generated per image under fixed seeds 42 through 46, giving 948 runs in total: 158 targeted occlusions and 790 controls.

Any occlusion changes the image, so a change in answer proves nothing on its own. The control is what makes this a test rather than an observation. Because the number of occluded pixels is held constant, a systematic difference between conditions cannot be attributed to the amount removed. Table~\ref{tab:maskchar} confirms the size match holds exactly and characterizes what else differs.

Two qualifications follow, and we prefer to state them plainly rather than let the averages carry more weight than they can.

First, the control does not isolate as much as it may appear. It does establish that the effect is not produced by removing pixels as such, since 88.2\% of random masks overlap the target at an IoU of 0.05 or below while occluding an identical area. It does not establish that the rule-relevant region is special among \textit{informative} regions, because a randomly placed mask disturbs content the pipeline was actually using only 1.4\% of the time, against 5.1\% for targeted masks. The comparison separates rule-relevant evidence from arbitrary image area, not from other meaningful content. A near-target control, placing an equally sized mask adjacent to but not overlapping the rule region, would tighten this and is the most valuable single extension to the protocol.

Second, mask areas are strongly right-skewed: the median target covers 1.4\% of the image, but 13 of 158 targets cover more than half of it. On those 13 images the control is geometrically degenerate, because a mask of equal size cannot avoid a box that large. Their random masks average a target IoU of 0.572 and none reaches the 0.05 criterion, against a mean of 0.009 and a 96.1\% success rate on the remaining 145 images. We therefore repeat the primary comparison with those images removed and report both results in Section~4, rather than relying on the pooled figure alone.

A further caveat concerns the mask itself. A black rectangle is not a natural occlusion, and the pipeline may respond partly to the artifact rather than to the absence of the evidence. Size matching controls for this to the extent that both conditions introduce an identical artifact, but it does not rule out an interaction between artifact and location.

The primary statistic is the \textit{answer-flip rate}: the proportion of images whose answer changes after occlusion. We compute it under both conditions and report the paired difference, defined per image as the targeted outcome minus the mean of that image's five controls. Pairing within image removes differences in scene difficulty. A paired difference near zero would indicate the pipeline is no more sensitive to rule-relevant evidence than to arbitrary pixels; a large positive difference indicates location-specific dependence.

Confidence intervals come from bootstrap resampling at the image level with 10{,}000 resamples under seed 20260716. Paired comparisons additionally use the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which does not assume the differences are normally distributed, with matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation as the effect size and Holm correction across the two primary paired tests.

The full configuration of the intervention study, covering model and dataset revisions, decoding settings, mask construction, placement search, seeds, and every statistical setting quoted above, is frozen in \texttt{analysis/protocol.json} in the released repository and is reproduced in the repository documentation.

Using this protocol, we now compare the evaluated pipeline in terms of answer performance, evidence quality, and evidence dependence.

\subsection{Answer Correctness (RQ1)}

The key comparison in Figure~\ref{fig:audit} is between the automated baseline and the grounding pipeline on the same 120 adjudicated rows. The automated baseline reaches 78.3\% accuracy, but this number should not be read as competence. That baseline is derived from the same dataset metadata used to assemble the candidate split, so it is partly scoring itself. Its macro-F1 of 50.5\% exposes the difference: it handles the majority violation class and fails elsewhere.

Florence-2 reaches 18.3\% accuracy and 12.5\% macro-F1 on this set. Two caveats keep this from being a general performance claim. First, the audit set was deliberately built from disagreement and weak-overlap cases, so it represents hard cases rather than average ones. On the full splits scored against the weaker metadata-assisted labels, the same pipeline reaches 41.7\% accuracy on the 163-row pilot and 39.3\% on the 588-row scale-up. Second, the low audit-set score is a statement about the complete pipeline, grounding plus rule logic, not about Florence-2's detection ability in isolation.

Accuracy is the wrong summary for a safety task, because it prices a missed hazard and a false alarm identically. Table~\ref{tab:errors} separates them. On these contested rows the pipeline reported \textit{compliant} for 70 of the 83 genuine violations, a missed-violation rate of 84.3\%, while also raising false alarms on 22 of 31 compliant rows. Both directions are poor, but they are not equally consequential: a false alarm costs an unnecessary inspection, whereas a missed violation is the failure the system exists to prevent. For comparison, the metadata-assisted baseline misses 13.3\% of violations on the same rows.

Two further patterns matter more than the headline number. The pipeline never returned \textit{uncertain} on any of the 120 audited rows, despite six rows being adjudicated as genuinely undecidable from a single frame. A system that cannot abstain and cannot flag a violation in an entire rule family is not merely inaccurate; it fails silently, in the direction that matters most for safety.

For comparison, three strategies that never look at the image at all provide the floor. A manifest-seed baseline that echoes the stored label scores 100\% by construction, confirming that portion of the annotation is circular. \subsection{Evidence Quality (RQ2)}

Correctness scores say nothing about whether the pipeline pointed at the right thing. Table~\ref{tab:rulelevel} answers that question, and the pattern to look for is the gap between accuracy and evidence presence within each rule family.

The pipeline almost always produces evidence: it returned at least one region for 96.9\% of pilot rows and 98.8\% of scale-up rows. The regions themselves, however, agree poorly with the rule's reference region. Mean best IoU is 0.369 on the pilot split and only 0.058 on the scale-up split. An IoU of 0.058 means the returned rectangle and the rule-relevant rectangle barely intersect.

The rule-level breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:rulelevel} explains the difference between the two splits and is itself the more informative result. Hard-hat rows dominate the scale-up split, 303 of 588, and carry the worst overlap at 0.030. Struck-by rows are similar at 0.033. These are exactly the rule families whose evidence is small or relational: a hard hat occupies a few dozen pixels on a worker's head, and equipment proximity is a relationship between two objects rather than a single object with a boundary. Guardrail and fall-protection evidence is physically larger, and overlap is correspondingly better at 0.171 and 0.130.

The practical consequence is specific. A safety reviewer using this pipeline would receive a bounding box on essentially every image, which looks like a system that always shows its work. On hard-hat rows that box would usually not contain the hard hat in question. Evidence presence is therefore not evidence quality, and an interface that displays model evidence without scoring it can create confidence that the underlying measurement does not support.

\subsection{Evidence Dependence (RQ3)}

The final question is whether the answer depends on the rule-relevant region at all. The comparison to read in Figure~\ref{fig:intervention} is targeted occlusion against size-matched random occlusion, since both remove identical numbers of pixels.

Occluding the rule-relevant region changed the answer 39.2\% of the time, against 9.2\% for size-matched random masks. The paired difference is 30.0 percentage points, with a bootstrap 95\% interval of 22.3 to 37.7 points and a rank-biserial effect size of 0.89. Evidence relocation shows the same pattern: the paired centroid-drift difference is 0.166, interval 0.140 to 0.193, Holm-adjusted $p < .001$.

Because the control degenerates on the 13 images whose target box covers more than half the frame, we repeat the comparison without them. The difference does not depend on those cases; it grows slightly, to 31.7 points with an interval of 23.7 to 39.9 across the remaining 145 images. This is the expected direction, since on degenerate images the ``random'' mask substantially overlaps the target and therefore flips answers too, compressing the gap. We report the pooled figure as the headline result because it is the more conservative of the two.

This result cuts in two directions, and both matter. Positively, the model is not merely reacting to generic construction-scene clutter. If it were, removing a rule-relevant region would be no more disruptive than removing an arbitrary region of the same size, and the paired difference would sit near zero. It does not. Something in the pipeline is genuinely location-sensitive.

Negatively, sensitivity is not the same as sound reasoning. When rule-relevant evidence disappears, the appropriate response for a safety system is to report that it can no longer tell. Instead the pipeline substitutes a different confident verdict, consistent with the complete absence of \textit{uncertain} answers reported under RQ1. In a deployed screening tool, that behavior means an occluded, dirty, or badly angled view of a worker does not produce a flag for human review; it produces a different answer, delivered with the same confidence as the original.

Read together, the three results are not redundant. The pipeline is often wrong (RQ1), usually points somewhere unhelpful (RQ2), and yet is measurably sensitive to the right pixels (RQ3). Any one of these measured alone would give a misleading impression of the system.

\subsection{Qualitative Cases}

Individual audit rows show what these aggregates look like in practice. In one PPE row, the adjudicated label is a violation because a third pedestrian in dark clothing is bare-headed while nearby workers wear yellow hard hats; a system drawn to the salient yellow helmets records compliance and misses the person actually at risk. In a struck-by row, a worker in high-visibility clothing stands near an operating excavator; the adjudicated label is compliant, but the single frame cannot confirm distance or swing state, which is precisely the situation an \textit{uncertain} answer exists to express.

These cases share a structure. The correct judgment depends on a small, specific, rule-defined region, while the surrounding image offers abundant construction-scene cues that point toward a plausible but unfounded answer.

\section{Discussion and Implications}

The central finding is that correctness, grounding, and evidence dependence dissociate. The audited pipeline is inaccurate on hard cases, returns evidence that overlaps the rule-relevant region poorly, and is nonetheless measurably more sensitive to that region than to equivalent irrelevant regions. A benchmark reporting only the first of these would call the system weak. One reporting only the third would call it evidence-driven. Both descriptions would be incomplete, and the safety-relevant behavior sits in the disagreement between them.

A plausible technical explanation lies in the difference between detecting an object and adjudicating a rule. Open-vocabulary grounding is trained to answer ``where is X,'' whereas a safety rule asks whether every worker in view satisfies a requirement, which is a quantified judgment that often turns on \textit{absence}: no hard hat on that head, no lanyard on that harness point, no barrier at that edge. A detector that fails to find an object cannot distinguish that failure from the object being absent, so the rule logic must adopt a default verdict for ungrounded cases. Whatever default it adopts then becomes the answer for every case where grounding fails, which is consistent with the degenerate all-compliant behavior we observe on the hard-hat and guardrail slices, precisely the families with the weakest evidence overlap.

For construction safety the implication is about failure mode rather than accuracy alone. A screening tool that misses hazards is dangerous, but a screening tool that misses hazards while displaying a confident verdict and a plausible bounding box is worse, because it supplies the visual trappings of justification. Our RQ2 result shows this concretely: a reviewer would receive a box on 98.8\% of images, and on hard-hat rows that box would usually not contain the relevant hard hat. The RQ3 result compounds it, since degrading the very evidence a rule depends on yields a different confident answer rather than a request for human attention. The safety-relevant property is not only how often a system is right, but whether it knows when it cannot tell.

These findings connect to two literatures. They extend to a safety-critical visual domain the established result that plausible-looking explanations need not reflect what a model uses \citep{jain2019attention,wu2024faithfulness} and that correct answers can be produced without visual grounding at all \citep{villa2025merlim}. Our contribution to that line is the rule-defined reference region: where hidden hallucinations have previously been identified against general visual content, a safety rule specifies in advance which region should be decisive, which makes the targeted intervention well posed and supports the argument for intervention-based rather than inspection-based faithfulness testing. They also qualify optimism about applying general-purpose VLMs to construction inspection \citep{chen2025inspectors}: our pipeline's difficulty is not primarily detection but the translation from detections into rule verdicts, which is where safety semantics live.

Two practical recommendations follow. First, evaluations of safety-critical VLMs should report an abstention rate alongside accuracy; a system that never returns ``uncertain'' should be treated as unvalidated for review support regardless of its headline score. Second, systems that display evidence regions to human reviewers should have those regions scored, not merely rendered, since unscored evidence invites reviewers to assume a grounding that has not been demonstrated.

We are correspondingly careful about what these results do not establish. They characterize one grounding pipeline on one dataset, and they do not rank VLMs, demonstrate that stronger instruction-following models share this behavior, or measure how human reviewers actually respond to evidence displays. The benchmark supports model comparison, regression testing, and audit documentation. It does not license autonomous inspection, worker discipline, or surveillance.

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}

The results above rest on a chain of choices, each of which could bear weight it cannot carry. We set out the load-bearing ones here.

\textbf{Model coverage.} One system was evaluated. Every empirical statement in this paper is a statement about that pipeline. We cannot distinguish failures specific to Florence-2 from failures of compact grounding models generally, or from failures of the detection-to-rule translation step that any such pipeline requires. No result here should be read as characterizing vision-language models as a class, and comparative claims need a wider model suite.

\textbf{Selection.} The audit set conditions on pipeline--baseline disagreement, so its statistics describe contested cases and not the benchmark as a whole. We report full-split figures alongside every audit-set figure for this reason, but the enrichment cannot be undone analytically, and an evaluation subset sampled without conditioning on disagreement would be a stronger design.

\textbf{Annotation.} Reference labels were produced by two AI passes plus adjudication, not by independent construction-safety professionals. The safety-judgment portion of the labelling task, as distinct from the perceptual portion, is the part least supported by this procedure, and it is the part that determines most reported accuracies.

\textbf{Intervention.} A black rectangle is not a natural occlusion. The pipeline may respond in part to the artifact rather than to the absence of evidence, and size matching equalizes but does not eliminate this, since artifact and location could interact.

\textbf{Control.} Size-matched random masks separate the rule-relevant region from arbitrary image area, but they disturb content the pipeline was using only 1.4\% of the time. They therefore do not establish that the rule region is special among informative regions. On the 13 images with very large targets the control degenerates entirely, which is why we report the comparison with those images excluded as well.

\textbf{Interpretation.} Intervention sensitivity is an observable behavior, not a demonstration of faithful reasoning. A changed answer shows dependence on the occluded location; it does not show that the system reasoned from that evidence in any richer sense. An unchanged answer is equally ambiguous, since redundant visual cues, language priors, or an ineffective mask would each produce one. We report sensitivity and let it constrain, rather than settle, the faithfulness question.

\textbf{Scope of measurement.} The benchmark does not measure human-like reasoning, internal chain-of-thought, causal reasoning in any strong sense, general model interpretability, deployment reliability, real-world injury reduction, or how human reviewers respond to displayed evidence. It measures three observable behaviors on a fixed set of images and rules, and it supports comparison between systems evaluated under the same protocol. It provides one mechanism for assessing evidence dependence within a broader trustworthy-AI evaluation programme; it does not constitute such a programme on its own.

\textbf{Scale and distribution.} The evaluation covers 163 pilot and 588 scale-up rows drawn from a single source collection. The distribution is sharply imbalanced: scanning 3,004 source rows yielded only 13 struck-by-risk and 75 fall-hazard candidates against 250 PPE-violation and 250 compliant candidates. Struck-by and fall-hazard results are therefore diagnostic rather than stable estimates, and future releases should deliberately oversample rare but safety-critical events.

\textbf{Rule coverage.} Four rule families are covered. Real safety programs encompass many more, including electrical, confined-space, excavation, and housekeeping hazards, and rules that depend on temporal context rather than a single frame.

\textbf{Static frames.} Every judgment is made from one image. Hazards defined by motion or duration, such as equipment swing radius or how long a worker remains exposed, cannot be assessed, and some rows are genuinely undecidable for this reason.

\textbf{Metric scope.} Evidence is compared at the level of rectangles. Box overlap does not capture whether the model used the correct part of a correctly located object, and our intervention measure detects location sensitivity rather than recovering internal reasoning, so it constrains a faithfulness assessment without settling it.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

Construction imagery is imagery of people at work, and the concerns it raises are concrete rather than abstract. A camera installed to check hard-hat compliance is also a camera that can support productivity monitoring, biometric identification, or retrospective review of an individual worker's conduct, and footage gathered for one purpose is routinely available for another. Safety monitoring does not by itself justify persistent worker surveillance; the two should be separated deliberately, through retention limits, restrictions on secondary use, meaningful consent, and worker representation in decisions about deployment. The benchmark does not infer identity, intent, competence, or blame, and it should not be used for autonomous inspection or disciplinary monitoring. Because the failure modes documented here fall hardest on missed violations, deploying such a system without human review would shift risk onto workers while presenting an appearance of oversight. Appropriate use is research evaluation under documented scope limits, data minimization, source-license compliance, and human professional review, consistent with risk-management guidance for AI-enabled workplace tools \citep{nist2023airmf,howard2024workplace}.

\section{Conclusion}

Construction safety depends on small, rule-specific visual details, and vision-language models are increasingly proposed to help check them at a scale human supervision cannot reach. Whether such a system can be trusted depends not only on how often it is right, but on whether its judgments rest on the visual evidence the rule actually concerns.

This paper introduced ConstructionSafety-FaithBench to measure that distinction, separating answer correctness, evidence grounding, and evidence dependence rather than collapsing them into one score. Auditing an open-vocabulary grounding pipeline across four construction rule families, we found the three come apart. The pipeline reached 18.3\% accuracy on an adjudicated set of hard cases, returned an evidence region on 98.8\% of images while overlapping the rule-relevant region with mean IoU of 0.058, and yet changed its answer 39.2\% of the time when rule-relevant evidence was occluded against 9.2\% for size-matched random occlusions, a paired difference of 30.0 points. It never once answered ``uncertain.''

For research practice, this argues that answer accuracy is insufficient evidence of trustworthiness in safety-critical visual applications, and that faithfulness claims require interventions with size-matched controls rather than inspection of plausible-looking explanations. For construction practice, it argues that a screening tool's willingness to abstain deserves as much scrutiny as its accuracy, because the dangerous failure is not an obvious error but a confident verdict accompanied by evidence no one has scored.

The main limitations are a single evaluated model, a single imagery source, and an audit-label layer produced by AI passes rather than certified safety professionals. Replacing that layer with domain-expert annotation, extending the model suite to stronger instruction-following VLMs, and testing how human reviewers actually respond to displayed evidence are the natural next steps. The broader point is that in safety-critical settings, knowing what a model looked at matters as much as knowing what it said.
