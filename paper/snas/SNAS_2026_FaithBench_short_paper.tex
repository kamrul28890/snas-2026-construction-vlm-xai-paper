\begin{abstract}

Construction safety depends on small, rule-specific visual details: a hard hat on a particular worker, a barrier at a particular edge. Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed to check such details automatically, but can give the right safety answer for the wrong visual reason, judging a scene unsafe because construction sites look hazardous while ignoring the cue the rule concerns. Existing evaluations report answer accuracy and are silent on this failure. We introduce ConstructionSafety-FaithBench, a model-agnostic audit benchmark separating three properties usually merged into one score: whether the answer is correct, whether the cited visual evidence is rule-relevant, and whether the answer changes when that evidence is removed, the last probed by occluding the rule-relevant region and comparing against size-matched occlusions placed elsewhere, so any difference reflects location rather than the amount removed. Validated on an open-vocabulary grounding pipeline built on Florence-2 across hard-hat, fall-protection, guardrail, and struck-by rules, the three come apart: 18.3\% accuracy on 120 adjudicated hard cases, an evidence region returned on 98.8\% of images yet overlapping the rule-relevant one at mean intersection-over-union 0.058, and answers flipping 39.2\% of the time under targeted occlusion against 9.2\% under matched controls, a paired difference of 30.0 points, with ``uncertain'' never once returned. Coming from one system on one imagery source, these findings characterize that pipeline rather than VLMs in general, but indicate that answer accuracy is insufficient evidence of trustworthiness for safety-critical screening, and that a system's willingness to abstain deserves scrutiny alongside its accuracy. Artifacts: \url{https://github.com/kamrul28890/snas-2026-construction-safety-faithbench}.

\section{Introduction}

Construction and extraction occupations accounted for 1,032 fatal work injuries in the United States in 2024, 370 of them falls, slips, and trips \citep{bls2026cfoi}, and the leading causes of construction death are falls, struck-by incidents, caught-in or caught-between events, and electrocution \citep{osha_focusfour}. Most such hazards are visible before they cause harm, so safety supervision is a visual task limited by coverage, and sites already produce ample imagery that could be checked automatically against written rules. Vision-language models (VLMs), which take an image with a natural-language prompt and return a natural-language response, are an appealing tool for this, and some also return the image regions they associate with that response. But safety-critical use changes what counts as an acceptable answer: a missed fall-protection violation can precede a fatal fall, and a confidently reported hazard that does not exist erodes the trust that makes the tool usable. A reviewer needs to know whether a verdict rests on the visual evidence the rule actually concerns.

Existing evaluation does not close this gap. Benchmarks for construction-safety VLMs report answer accuracy, which is silent on whether the answer used the right evidence \citep{chen2025inspectors}, and broader work on rationale quality is not specific to safety rules or their localized cues \citep{li2025rationales}. MERLIM identifies \textit{hidden hallucinations}, correct answers produced without meaningful visual grounding, reporting that instruction-tuned models often succeed by exploiting scene patterns and language priors \citep{villa2025merlim}; it establishes this on general imagery but not where a written rule designates which region is decisive, the situation a safety inspection creates. Methodologically, internal scores such as attention weights can look convincing while failing to track what a model uses \citep{jain2019attention,wu2024faithfulness}, motivating tests of behavior under controlled intervention and aligning with the requirements set out for explainable systems \citep{phillips2021principles}.

This paper separates three properties that a single accuracy score merges. They come apart in practice: a system can be correct without being grounded, inferring ``unsafe'' from generic scene clutter, and can return a plausible-looking region that does not drive its answer. We ask:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (Correctness).} How accurately does the evaluated grounding pipeline answer construction-safety rule questions, scored against adjudicated reference labels?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (Evidence relevance).} Does the visual evidence the pipeline returns correspond to the region the safety rule actually concerns?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (Intervention sensitivity).} Does removing the rule-relevant region change the pipeline's answer more than removing an equally sized region elsewhere in the same image?
\end{itemize}

None of the three establishes \textit{faithfulness}, the stronger property that an answer genuinely depends on the evidence that ought to support it. Intervention sensitivity is necessary but not sufficient: a changed answer shows dependence on the occluded location, yet a black rectangle also introduces an artifact the system may react to, and an unchanged answer may reflect redundant cues or language priors rather than absent dependence. We therefore report sensitivity as an observable behavior and treat faithfulness as an interpretive question this evidence informs without resolving.

To answer them we introduce ConstructionSafety-FaithBench, an audit benchmark pairing each image with a written safety rule, an answer label, the rule-relevant evidence regions, and label provenance, across four rule families matched to the leading hazard categories: harness and fall protection, guardrail and edge protection, struck-by hazards from equipment proximity, and hard-hat and personal protective equipment (PPE) compliance. Schema, metrics, and protocol are model-agnostic, and any system returning an answer with evidence regions can be run through them; the empirical results come from a single system, so findings are phrased as properties of the evaluated pipeline rather than of the model class.

\section{Benchmark and Audit Pipeline}

\textbf{Images and safety rules.} The benchmark is built from ConstructionSite imagery, released with safety-oriented captioning, rule-violation, and object-grounding annotations \citep{chen2025inspectors} and chosen because generic object-detection corpora do not carry safety semantics. Each row records the source split, the safety question, a normalized rule identifier, the answer label, the rule-relevant evidence regions as \textit{bounding boxes}, and label provenance. Answers are \textit{compliant}, \textit{violation}, or \textit{uncertain}, the last meaning the image cannot support a decision; that third label matters because a system forced to choose between the first two will guess when it should defer. A 163-row pilot split supports the intervention study and a 588-row scale-up split tests consistency at larger scale.

\textbf{The evaluated pipeline.} The system under audit locates candidate objects, then applies a fixed rule to those locations. Locating uses the \textit{microsoft/Florence-2-base-ft} release of Florence-2, which returns bounding boxes for the objects a text prompt describes \citep{xiao2024florence}, a capability called \textit{open-vocabulary grounding}: the model is not restricted to trained categories, so it can be asked for ``hard hat'' or ``guardrail'' in words, without retraining per rule, and is small enough to run repeatedly on modest hardware as the intervention study requires. Around it a \textit{grounding adapter} applies fixed geometric tests to the returned boxes, checking for a hard-hat rule whether a helmet box overlaps each detected worker's head region. These tests are \textit{deterministic}, so any failure traces to the grounding step or the rule logic rather than hiding inside a second black box. Three blind reference points bound the problem from below: \textit{manifest-seed} echoes the stored label, \textit{majority-violation} always answers ``violation,'' and \textit{caption-keyword} reads only the caption. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the full path, and every stage writes normalized records so each reported number traces back to the run that produced it.

\textbf{The audit label layer.} Reference labels are built in three layers, and their quality bounds what the evaluation can claim. Automated baseline annotations from the dataset's own metadata scale cheaply but derive from the same metadata used to assemble the splits, so they are never treated as independent ground truth. A prioritized audit set of 120 rows is drawn by conditioning on pipeline--baseline disagreement, where expensive adjudication is most informative; 118 of the 120 are outright answer disagreements. \textbf{The audit set is therefore enriched for disagreement and is not a representative sample}, so its statistics estimate performance on contested cases and every audit-set figure below is reported alongside the corresponding full-split figure. Each audited row then receives two independent passes, with the 12 disagreements resolved by a returned adjudication step. The passes agree on 108 of 120 rows, but raw agreement flatters a set in which one label dominates, so we report Cohen's $\kappa = 0.78$ against an expected agreement of 0.54: substantial consistency in applying the protocol, not evidence that the protocol is correct, because both passes were generated by AI annotators working from the same written instructions rather than by independent human domain experts.

We therefore call these \textit{reference labels} rather than ground truth. The task mixes perceptual judgments a careful non-expert can answer reliably, such as whether a helmet is visible on a head, with safety judgments depending on training and site experience, such as whether a proximity to operating equipment is unsafe; only the first is well supported by our procedure, and the six rows adjudicated \textit{uncertain} concentrate in the judgment-dependent cases. That these labels lack validation by independent construction-safety professionals is the single largest threat to every accuracy figure we report.

\section{Metrics and Experimental Setup}

Each research question requires a different measurement, and the central design decision of this benchmark is to keep them apart.

\textbf{Answer metrics (RQ1).} \textit{Accuracy}, the proportion of rows whose answer matches the audit label, is misleading under class imbalance: most audited rows are violations, so always answering ``violation'' scores well without understanding anything. \textit{Macro-F1}, the equally weighted average of the per-class F1 scores, corrects for this, and the gap between the two is diagnostic of a system exploiting the label distribution rather than reading the image.

\textbf{Evidence metrics (RQ2).} \textit{Evidence presence} records whether any region was returned at all, which matters because a system that silently returns nothing cannot be audited. \textit{Box overlap} compares the returned and reference regions by intersection over union (IoU), the area two rectangles share divided by the area they jointly cover. \textit{Normalized centroid drift} measures how far the returned region's center moves as a fraction of the image diagonal, so it is comparable across image sizes; we report it alongside IoU because the two disagree on small objects, where a modest shift destroys IoU while barely moving the centroid.

\label{sec:intervention}
\textbf{Intervention metrics (RQ3).} Faithfulness cannot be read off a single run, because a correct answer and a well-placed box are both compatible with the model ignoring the evidence entirely, so we intervene on the image and observe what changes. For each image a \textit{targeted occlusion} places a solid black rectangle over the first rule-relevant object box in the manifest: the head region for hard-hat rows, the exposed edge for guardrails, the worker-at-height region for fall protection, and the worker--equipment proximity region for struck-by risk. Each matched-random control uses identical width and height at a seeded random location chosen to keep target overlap at or below 0.05 IoU, falling back to the lowest-overlap candidate rather than discarding the image. Five controls per image give 948 runs: 158 targeted and 790 control.

Any occlusion changes the image, so a change in answer proves nothing on its own; the control is what makes this a test. Because occluded area is held constant, mean share 0.136 and median 0.014 in both conditions, a systematic difference cannot be attributed to the amount removed, and the conditions differ in placement rather than extent (mask--target IoU 1.000 targeted against 0.056 matched-random).

The control isolates less than it may appear. It establishes that the effect is not produced by removing pixels as such, since 88.2\% of random masks overlap the target at an IoU of 0.05 or below while occluding an identical area, but not that the rule-relevant region is special among \textit{informative} regions: a random mask disturbs content the pipeline was using only 1.4\% of the time against 5.1\% for targeted masks, so the comparison separates rule-relevant evidence from arbitrary image area rather than from other meaningful content. A near-target control is the most valuable single extension to the protocol. Two further caveats, with supporting statistics in the released scripts: the control degenerates on the 13 of 158 targets covering more than half the image, so we repeat the comparison without them; and a black rectangle is not a natural occlusion, which size matching equalizes but does not eliminate.

The primary statistic is the \textit{answer-flip rate}, the proportion of images whose answer changes after occlusion, reported as a paired difference per image against the mean of that image's five controls, so pairing removes differences in scene difficulty. Intervals come from image-level bootstrap resampling with 10{,}000 resamples, and paired comparisons use the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test with rank-biserial effect size and Holm correction. The full configuration is frozen in \texttt{analysis/protocol.json} in the released repository.

\textbf{Answer correctness (RQ1).} On the 120 adjudicated rows, of which 83 are violations, 31 compliant, and 6 uncertain, the automated baseline reaches 78.3\% accuracy. That is not competence: it derives from the same metadata used to assemble the candidate split, so it is partly scoring itself, and its macro-F1 of 50.5\% exposes the difference. The two audit passes themselves score 93.3\% and 95.8\% against the final labels.

Florence-2 reaches 18.3\% accuracy and 12.5\% macro-F1 here. Two caveats keep this from being a general performance claim: the audit set was built from disagreement and weak-overlap cases, and on the full splits scored against the weaker metadata-assisted labels the same pipeline reaches 41.7\% on the pilot and 39.3\% on the scale-up; and the score describes the complete pipeline, grounding plus rule logic, not Florence-2's detection in isolation.

Accuracy prices a missed hazard and a false alarm identically, so the third block of Table~\ref{tab:headline} separates them: the pipeline reported \textit{compliant} for 70 of 83 genuine violations, a missed-violation rate of 84.3\%, while raising false alarms on 22 of 31 compliant rows, against 13.3\% missed for the metadata-assisted baseline. A false alarm costs an unnecessary inspection; a missed violation is the failure the system exists to prevent.

Two patterns matter more than the headline number. The pipeline never returned \textit{uncertain} on any of the 120 rows, despite six being adjudicated undecidable from a single frame, and on the hard-hat and guardrail slices it answered \textit{compliant} for all 30 rows in each, though 26 and 24 respectively were violations. A system that cannot abstain and cannot flag a violation in an entire rule family fails silently, in the direction that matters most for safety. Three blind baselines set the floor: manifest-seed scores 100\% by construction, confirming that portion of the annotation is circular; majority-violation reaches 69.3\% on the pilot and 57.5\% on the scale-up; and caption-keyword reaches 19.7\%, so labels are not recoverable from captions alone. Answering ``violation'' every time therefore outperforms the grounding pipeline.

\textbf{Evidence quality (RQ2).} Correctness says nothing about whether the pipeline pointed at the right thing. It almost always produces evidence, returning at least one region for 96.9\% of pilot and 98.8\% of scale-up rows, but those regions agree poorly with the rule's reference region: mean best IoU is 0.369 on the pilot and only 0.058 on the scale-up split, meaning the returned and rule-relevant rectangles barely intersect.

The rule-level breakdown explains the gap and is the more informative result. Hard-hat rows dominate the scale-up split, 303 of 588, with the worst overlap at 0.030, and struck-by rows are similar at 0.033: exactly the families whose evidence is small or relational, since a hard hat occupies a few dozen pixels and equipment proximity is a relationship between two objects rather than one object with a boundary. Guardrail and fall-protection evidence is physically larger and overlaps better, at 0.171 and 0.130. Evidence presence exceeds 96\% in every family while per-family accuracy ranges from 63.6\% on guardrails down to 25.5\% on struck-by rows, so presence and correctness move independently.

The consequence is specific: a reviewer would receive a box on essentially every image, which looks like a system that always shows its work, yet on hard-hat rows that box would usually not contain the hard hat in question. Evidence presence is not evidence quality, and displaying model evidence without scoring it creates confidence the measurement does not support.

\textbf{Evidence dependence (RQ3).} The comparison is targeted against size-matched random occlusion, since both remove identical numbers of pixels. Occluding the rule-relevant region changed the answer 39.2\% of the time against 9.2\% for matched controls: a paired difference of 30.0 percentage points, bootstrap 95\% interval 22.3 to 37.7, rank-biserial effect size 0.89. Evidence relocation shows the same pattern, with a paired centroid-drift difference of 0.166, interval 0.140 to 0.193, Holm-adjusted $p < .001$. Excluding the 13 images where the control degenerates, the difference grows slightly to 31.7 points across the remaining 145; we report the pooled figure as the more conservative of the two.

This cuts both ways. The model is not merely reacting to generic scene clutter, or the paired difference would sit near zero. But sensitivity is not sound reasoning: when rule-relevant evidence disappears, the appropriate response for a safety system is to report that it can no longer tell, and instead the pipeline substitutes a different confident verdict, consistent with the absence of \textit{uncertain} answers under RQ1. In deployment, an occluded or badly angled view of a worker would not produce a flag for human review but a different answer, delivered with the same confidence as the original.

The three results are therefore not redundant: the pipeline is often wrong (RQ1), usually points somewhere unhelpful (RQ2), and is nonetheless measurably sensitive to the right pixels (RQ3). Any one measured alone would mislead.

Individual rows in the released audit set show the same structure concretely, as when a bare-headed pedestrian in dark clothing is missed beside workers in salient yellow hard hats: the correct judgment turns on a small, rule-defined region while the surrounding image offers abundant cues pointing toward a plausible but unfounded answer.

\section{Discussion and Implications}

Correctness, grounding, and evidence dependence dissociate: a benchmark reporting only the first would call this system weak, one reporting only the third would call it evidence-driven, and the safety-relevant behavior sits in the disagreement. A plausible explanation is the difference between detecting an object and adjudicating a rule. Open-vocabulary grounding answers ``where is X,'' whereas a safety rule asks whether every worker satisfies a requirement, a quantified judgment that often turns on \textit{absence}: no hard hat on that head, no barrier at that edge. A detector that fails to find an object cannot distinguish that failure from absence, so the rule logic adopts a default verdict for ungrounded cases, and that default then answers every case where grounding fails, consistent with the all-compliant behavior on the hard-hat and guardrail slices, precisely the families with the weakest overlap.

The implication concerns failure mode rather than accuracy. A screening tool that misses hazards is dangerous; one that misses them while displaying a confident verdict and a plausible box is worse, because it supplies the visual trappings of justification. What matters is not only how often a system is right but whether it knows when it cannot tell. Two recommendations follow: report an abstention rate alongside accuracy, treating a system that never returns ``uncertain'' as unvalidated for review support; and score displayed evidence regions rather than merely rendering them, since unscored evidence invites reviewers to assume a grounding that has not been demonstrated.

These findings extend to a safety-critical visual domain the established results that plausible-looking explanations need not reflect what a model uses \citep{jain2019attention,wu2024faithfulness} and that correct answers can be produced without visual grounding \citep{villa2025merlim}. Our addition is the rule-defined reference region: a safety rule specifies in advance which region should be decisive, which makes the targeted intervention well posed. They also qualify optimism about general-purpose VLMs for construction inspection \citep{chen2025inspectors}, since the difficulty is not detection but the translation from detections into rule verdicts, where safety semantics live.

\section{Limitations and Ethical Considerations}

\textbf{Model and annotation.} One system was evaluated, so every empirical statement here describes that pipeline rather than compact grounding models generally, and comparative claims need a wider suite. Reference labels come from two AI passes plus adjudication, not from independent construction-safety professionals; the safety-judgment portion of that task is least supported by the procedure and determines most reported accuracies.

\textbf{Selection and scale.} The audit set conditions on pipeline--baseline disagreement, so its statistics describe contested cases; full-split figures accompany every audit-set figure, but the enrichment cannot be undone analytically. Coverage is 163 pilot and 588 scale-up rows from one collection and is sharply imbalanced: scanning 3,004 source rows yielded only 13 struck-by-risk and 75 fall-hazard candidates against 250 PPE-violation and 250 compliant, so those two families are diagnostic rather than stable estimates. Site types, regions, weather, and equipment conventions are not systematically varied, four rule families stand in for safety programs encompassing many more, and every judgment is made from a single frame, leaving hazards defined by motion or duration genuinely undecidable.

\textbf{Intervention and interpretation.} As Section~\ref{sec:intervention} sets out, a black rectangle is not a natural occlusion, the controls separate rule-relevant evidence from arbitrary image area rather than from other informative content, and they degenerate on the 13 largest targets. Sensitivity is an observable behavior, not a demonstration of faithful reasoning: a changed answer shows dependence on the occluded location and an unchanged one is equally ambiguous. Evidence is also compared only at the level of rectangles, which cannot capture whether the model used the correct part of a correctly located object.

\textbf{Scope.} The benchmark does not measure human-like or causal reasoning, internal chain-of-thought, general interpretability, deployment reliability, injury reduction, or how reviewers respond to displayed evidence. It measures three observable behaviors on a fixed set of images and rules; it is one mechanism within a trustworthy-AI evaluation programme, not such a programme on its own.

\textbf{Ethics and appropriate use.} A camera installed to check hard-hat compliance can equally support productivity monitoring, biometric identification, or retrospective review of a worker's conduct, and footage gathered for one purpose is routinely available for another. Safety monitoring does not justify persistent worker surveillance; the two should be separated through retention limits, restrictions on secondary use, meaningful consent, and worker representation in deployment decisions. The benchmark infers no identity, intent, competence, or blame, and should not be used for autonomous inspection or disciplinary monitoring: because the failure modes documented here fall hardest on missed violations, deploying such a system without human review would shift risk onto workers while presenting an appearance of oversight. Appropriate use is research evaluation under documented scope limits, data minimization, and human professional review, consistent with risk-management guidance for AI-enabled workplace tools \citep{nist2023airmf,howard2024workplace}.

\section{Conclusion}

ConstructionSafety-FaithBench separates answer correctness, evidence grounding, and evidence dependence rather than collapsing them into one score. Auditing an open-vocabulary grounding pipeline across four construction rule families, the three came apart: 18.3\% accuracy on adjudicated hard cases, an evidence region on 98.8\% of images overlapping the rule-relevant region at mean IoU 0.058, and answers changing 39.2\% of the time under targeted occlusion against 9.2\% under size-matched controls, with ``uncertain'' never once returned. Answer accuracy is therefore insufficient evidence of trustworthiness in safety-critical visual applications, faithfulness claims require interventions with size-matched controls rather than inspection of plausible explanations, and a screening tool's willingness to abstain deserves as much scrutiny as its accuracy, because the dangerous failure is not an obvious error but a confident verdict accompanied by evidence no one has scored. Replacing the AI-pass audit layer with domain-expert annotation, extending the model suite, and testing how reviewers respond to displayed evidence are the natural next steps.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} The benchmark, evaluation code, frozen model outputs, audit labels, intervention specifications, score tables, and analysis scripts are at \url{https://github.com/kamrul28890/snas-2026-construction-safety-faithbench}. Every number reported here is regenerated from those artifacts rather than transcribed by hand, and reproducing them requires neither the source images nor a GPU.
