\begin{abstract}

Construction safety depends on small, rule-specific visual details: a hard hat on a particular worker, a barrier at a particular edge. Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed to check such details automatically, but can give the right safety answer for the wrong visual reason, judging a scene unsafe because construction sites look hazardous while ignoring the cue the rule concerns. Existing evaluations report answer accuracy and are silent on this failure. We introduce ConstructionSafety-FaithBench, a model-agnostic audit benchmark separating three properties usually merged into one score: whether the answer is correct, whether the cited evidence is rule-relevant, and whether the answer changes when that evidence is removed, the last probed by occluding the rule-relevant region and comparing against size-matched occlusions elsewhere, so any difference reflects location rather than amount. Validated on an open-vocabulary grounding pipeline built on Florence-2 across four construction rule families, the three come apart: 18.3\% accuracy on 120 adjudicated hard cases, an evidence region on 98.8\% of images yet overlapping the rule-relevant one at mean intersection-over-union 0.058, and answers flipping 39.2\% of the time under targeted occlusion against 9.2\% under matched controls, with ``uncertain'' never once returned. These findings characterize one pipeline rather than VLMs in general, but indicate that answer accuracy is insufficient evidence of trustworthiness for safety-critical screening, and that a system's willingness to abstain deserves scrutiny alongside its accuracy. Artifacts: \url{https://github.com/kamrul28890/snas-2026-construction-safety-faithbench}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Construction and extraction occupations accounted for 1,032 fatal work injuries in the United States in 2024, 370 of them falls, slips, and trips \citep{bls2026cfoi}, and the leading causes of construction death are falls, struck-by incidents, caught-in or caught-between events, and electrocution \citep{osha_focusfour}. Most are visible before they cause harm, so supervision is a visual task limited by coverage. Vision-language models (VLMs), which answer a prompt about an image and can also return the regions they associate with that answer, are an appealing way to screen the imagery sites already produce. But safety-critical use changes what counts as acceptable: a missed fall-protection violation can precede a fatal fall, and a confidently reported hazard that does not exist erodes the trust that makes the tool usable. A reviewer needs to know whether a verdict rests on the evidence the rule concerns.

Existing evaluation does not close this gap. Benchmarks for construction-safety VLMs report answer accuracy, silent on whether the answer used the right evidence \citep{chen2025inspectors}, and broader work on rationale quality is not specific to safety rules or their localized cues \citep{li2025rationales}. MERLIM identifies \textit{hidden hallucinations}, correct answers produced without meaningful visual grounding, reporting that instruction-tuned models often exploit scene patterns and language priors \citep{villa2025merlim}; it establishes this on general imagery but not where a written rule designates which region is decisive. Internal scores such as attention weights likewise look convincing while failing to track what a model uses \citep{jain2019attention,wu2024faithfulness}, motivating intervention-based tests and aligning with requirements for explainable systems \citep{phillips2021principles}.

This paper separates three properties that a single accuracy score merges, and which come apart in practice: a system can be correct without being grounded, inferring ``unsafe'' from generic scene clutter, and can return a plausible-looking region that does not drive its answer. We ask:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt,parsep=0pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{RQ1 (Correctness).} How accurately does the pipeline answer safety-rule questions against adjudicated reference labels?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (Evidence relevance).} Does the evidence it returns correspond to the region the rule concerns?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (Intervention sensitivity).} Does removing the rule-relevant region change its answer more than removing an equally sized region elsewhere?
\end{itemize}

None of the three establishes \textit{faithfulness}, the stronger property that an answer genuinely depends on the evidence that ought to support it. Intervention sensitivity is necessary but not sufficient: a black rectangle introduces an artifact the system may react to, and an unchanged answer may reflect redundant cues or language priors rather than absent dependence. We therefore report sensitivity as an observable behavior and treat faithfulness as a question this evidence informs without resolving.

To answer them we introduce ConstructionSafety-FaithBench, an audit benchmark pairing each image with a written safety rule, an answer label, the rule-relevant evidence regions, and label provenance, across four rule families matched to the leading hazard categories: fall protection, guardrail and edge protection, struck-by hazards from equipment proximity, and hard-hat and personal protective equipment (PPE) compliance. Schema, metrics, and protocol are model-agnostic; the results come from a single system, so findings are phrased as properties of the evaluated pipeline, not the model class.

\section{Benchmark and Audit Pipeline}

\textbf{Images and safety rules.} The benchmark is built from ConstructionSite imagery, released with safety-oriented captioning, rule-violation, and object-grounding annotations \citep{chen2025inspectors} and chosen because generic object-detection corpora do not carry safety semantics. Each row records the split, the safety question, a rule identifier, the answer label, the rule-relevant evidence regions as \textit{bounding boxes}, and label provenance. Answers are \textit{compliant}, \textit{violation}, or \textit{uncertain}; the third matters because a system forced to choose between the first two will guess when it should defer. A 163-row pilot split supports the intervention study and a 588-row scale-up split tests consistency at scale.

\textbf{The evaluated pipeline.} The system under audit locates candidate objects, then applies a fixed rule to those locations. Locating uses the \textit{microsoft/Florence-2-base-ft} release of Florence-2, which returns bounding boxes for the objects a text prompt describes \citep{xiao2024florence}, a capability called \textit{open-vocabulary grounding}: it is not restricted to trained categories, so it can be asked for ``hard hat'' or ``guardrail'' in words, and is small enough to run repeatedly. Around it a \textit{grounding adapter} applies fixed geometric tests to the returned boxes, checking for a hard-hat rule whether a helmet box overlaps each detected worker's head region. These tests are \textit{deterministic}, so any failure traces to grounding or rule logic rather than hiding inside a second black box. Three blind reference points bound the problem from below: \textit{manifest-seed} echoes the stored label, \textit{majority-violation} always answers ``violation,'' \textit{caption-keyword} reads only the caption. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the path.

\textbf{The audit label layer.} Reference labels are built in three layers, and their quality bounds what the evaluation can claim. Automated baseline annotations derive from the same dataset metadata used to assemble the splits, so they are never treated as independent ground truth. A prioritized audit set of 120 rows is drawn by conditioning on pipeline--baseline disagreement, where adjudication is most informative; 118 are outright answer disagreements. \textbf{The audit set is therefore enriched for disagreement and is not a representative sample}, so its statistics estimate performance on contested cases and every audit-set figure below is reported alongside the full-split figure. Each audited row receives two independent passes, the 12 disagreements resolved by returned adjudication. The passes agree on 108 of 120 rows, but raw agreement flatters a set in which one label dominates, so we report Cohen's $\kappa = 0.78$ against an expected 0.54: consistency in applying the protocol, not evidence that it is correct, because both passes were generated by AI annotators from the same written instructions rather than by independent human domain experts.

We therefore call these \textit{reference labels} rather than ground truth. The task mixes perceptual judgments a careful non-expert can answer reliably, such as whether a helmet is visible on a head, with safety judgments depending on training and site experience; only the first is well supported by our procedure, and the six \textit{uncertain} rows concentrate in the judgment-dependent cases. Their lack of validation by independent construction-safety professionals is the single largest threat to every accuracy figure we report.

\section{Metrics and Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Answer and evidence metrics (RQ1, RQ2).} \textit{Accuracy}, the proportion of rows whose answer matches the audit label, is misleading under class imbalance: most audited rows are violations, so always answering ``violation'' scores well without understanding anything. \textit{Macro-F1}, the equally weighted average of per-class F1 scores, corrects for this, and the gap between them is diagnostic of a system exploiting the label distribution. For evidence, \textit{presence} records whether any region was returned at all, since a system that returns nothing cannot be audited; \textit{box overlap} compares returned and reference regions by intersection over union (IoU); and \textit{normalized centroid drift} measures how far the returned region's center moves as a fraction of the image diagonal, reported alongside IoU because a modest shift destroys IoU on small objects while barely moving the centroid.

\label{sec:intervention}
\textbf{Intervention metrics (RQ3).} A correct answer and a well-placed box are both compatible with the model ignoring the evidence entirely, so we intervene and observe what changes. For each image a \textit{targeted occlusion} places a solid black rectangle over the first rule-relevant object box in the manifest: the head region for hard hats, the exposed edge for guardrails, the worker-at-height region for fall protection, the worker--equipment region for struck-by risk. Each matched-random control uses identical dimensions at a seeded location chosen to keep target overlap at or below 0.05 IoU. Five controls per image give 948 runs: 158 targeted and 790 control.

Because occluded area is held constant (mean share 0.136, median 0.014 in both conditions), a systematic difference cannot be attributed to the amount removed; the conditions differ in placement, not extent (mask--target IoU 1.000 against 0.056).

The control isolates less than it may appear. It establishes that the effect is not produced by removing pixels as such, since 88.2\% of random masks overlap the target at an IoU of 0.05 or below while occluding identical area, but not that the rule-relevant region is special among \textit{informative} regions: a random mask disturbs content the pipeline was using only 1.4\% of the time against 5.1\% for targeted masks, so the comparison separates rule-relevant evidence from arbitrary image area rather than from other meaningful content, and a near-target control is the most valuable single extension. The control also degenerates on the 13 of 158 targets covering more than half the image, so we repeat the comparison without them; and a black rectangle is not a natural occlusion, which size matching equalizes but does not eliminate.

The primary statistic is the \textit{answer-flip rate}, the proportion of images whose answer changes after occlusion, reported as a paired difference per image against the mean of that image's five controls. Intervals come from image-level bootstrap resampling with 10{,}000 resamples, and paired comparisons use the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test with rank-biserial effect size and Holm correction.

\textbf{Answer correctness (RQ1).} On the 120 adjudicated rows the automated baseline reaches 78.3\% accuracy. That is not competence: it derives from the same metadata used to assemble the candidate split, so it is partly scoring itself, and its macro-F1 of 50.5\% exposes the difference. The two audit passes score 93.3\% and 95.8\% against the final labels. Florence-2 reaches 18.3\% accuracy and 12.5\% macro-F1. Two caveats keep this from being a general performance claim: the audit set was built from disagreement and weak-overlap cases, and on the full splits scored against the weaker metadata-assisted labels the same pipeline reaches 41.7\% on the pilot and 39.3\% on the scale-up; and the score describes the complete pipeline, grounding plus rule logic, not Florence-2's detection in isolation.

Accuracy prices a missed hazard and a false alarm identically, so the third block of Table~\ref{tab:headline} separates them: the pipeline reported \textit{compliant} for 70 of 83 genuine violations, a missed-violation rate of 84.3\%, while raising false alarms on 22 of 31 compliant rows, against 13.3\% missed for the metadata-assisted baseline. A false alarm costs an unnecessary inspection; a missed violation is the failure the system exists to prevent.

Two patterns matter more than the headline number. The pipeline never returned \textit{uncertain} on any of the 120 rows, despite six being adjudicated undecidable from a single frame, and on the hard-hat and guardrail slices it answered \textit{compliant} for all 30 rows in each, though 26 and 24 were violations. A system that cannot abstain and cannot flag a violation in an entire rule family fails silently, in the direction that matters most for safety. Three blind baselines set the floor: manifest-seed scores 100\% by construction, confirming that portion of the annotation is circular; majority-violation reaches 69.3\% and 57.5\% on the two splits; caption-keyword 19.7\%. Answering ``violation'' every time therefore outperforms the pipeline.

\textbf{Evidence quality (RQ2).} Correctness says nothing about whether the pipeline pointed at the right thing. It almost always produces evidence, returning at least one region for 96.9\% of pilot and 98.8\% of scale-up rows, but those regions agree poorly with the rule's reference region: mean best IoU is 0.369 on the pilot and only 0.058 on the scale-up split, so the returned and rule-relevant rectangles barely intersect.

The rule-level breakdown explains the gap and is the more informative result. Hard-hat rows dominate the scale-up split, 303 of 588, with the worst overlap at 0.030, and struck-by rows are similar at 0.033: exactly the families whose evidence is small or relational, since a hard hat occupies a few dozen pixels and equipment proximity is a relationship rather than one object with a boundary. Guardrail and fall-protection evidence is larger and overlaps better, at 0.171 and 0.130. Evidence presence exceeds 96\% in every family while per-family accuracy ranges from 63.6\% to 25.5\%, so presence and correctness move independently. A reviewer would therefore receive a box on essentially every image, yet on hard-hat rows it would usually not contain the hard hat. Evidence presence is not evidence quality, and displaying evidence without scoring it creates confidence the measurement does not support.

\textbf{Evidence dependence (RQ3).} Occluding the rule-relevant region changed the answer 39.2\% of the time against 9.2\% for size-matched controls, a paired difference of 30.0 points with bootstrap 95\% interval 22.3 to 37.7 and rank-biserial effect size 0.89; evidence relocation shows the same pattern, paired centroid-drift difference 0.166, interval 0.140 to 0.193, Holm-adjusted $p < .001$. Excluding the 13 images where the control degenerates, the difference grows to 31.7 points across the remaining 145; we report the pooled figure as the more conservative.

This cuts both ways. The model is not merely reacting to generic scene clutter, or the paired difference would sit near zero. But sensitivity is not sound reasoning: when rule-relevant evidence disappears the appropriate response is to report that it can no longer tell, and instead the pipeline substitutes a different confident verdict, consistent with the absence of \textit{uncertain} answers under RQ1. In deployment, an occluded or badly angled view would not produce a flag for review but a different answer at the same confidence.

The three results are therefore not redundant: the pipeline is often wrong (RQ1), usually points somewhere unhelpful (RQ2), and is nonetheless sensitive to the right pixels (RQ3).

The released audit set shows this concretely: a bare-headed pedestrian missed beside workers in salient yellow hard hats, where the judgment turns on a small rule-defined region amid abundant misleading cues.

\section{Discussion and Implications}

Correctness, grounding, and evidence dependence dissociate: a benchmark reporting only the first would call this system weak, one reporting only the third would call it evidence-driven, and the safety-relevant behavior sits in the disagreement. The likely cause is the difference between detecting an object and adjudicating a rule. Grounding answers ``where is X,'' whereas a rule asks whether every worker satisfies a requirement, a judgment that often turns on \textit{absence}: no hard hat on that head, no barrier at that edge. A detector that fails to find an object cannot distinguish that failure from absence, so the rule logic adopts a default verdict that then answers every ungrounded case, consistent with the all-compliant behavior on the hard-hat and guardrail slices.

The implication concerns failure mode rather than accuracy. A tool that misses hazards is dangerous; one that misses them while displaying a confident verdict and a plausible box is worse, because it supplies the visual trappings of justification. Two recommendations follow: report an abstention rate alongside accuracy, treating a system that never returns ``uncertain'' as unvalidated for review support; and score displayed evidence regions rather than merely rendering them, since unscored evidence invites reviewers to assume a grounding never demonstrated.

These findings extend to a safety-critical domain the established results that plausible explanations need not reflect what a model uses \citep{jain2019attention,wu2024faithfulness} and that correct answers can be produced without visual grounding \citep{villa2025merlim}. Our addition is the rule-defined reference region, which specifies in advance which region should be decisive and makes the intervention well posed. They also qualify optimism about general-purpose VLMs for construction inspection \citep{chen2025inspectors}: the difficulty is not detection but translating detections into rule verdicts.

\section{Limitations and Ethical Considerations}

\textbf{Model and annotation.} One system was evaluated, so every empirical statement describes that pipeline, not compact grounding models in general. Reference labels come from two AI passes plus adjudication, not from independent construction-safety professionals; the safety-judgment portion of that task is least supported by the procedure and determines most reported accuracies.

\textbf{Selection and scale.} The audit set's enrichment for disagreement cannot be undone analytically. Coverage is 163 pilot and 588 scale-up rows from one collection and is sharply imbalanced: scanning 3,004 source rows yielded only 13 struck-by-risk and 75 fall-hazard candidates against 250 PPE-violation and 250 compliant, so those families are diagnostic rather than stable estimates. Site types, regions, and equipment conventions are not systematically varied, four rule families stand in for programs encompassing many more, and single-frame judgment leaves hazards defined by motion or duration undecidable.

\textbf{Intervention and interpretation.} As Section~\ref{sec:intervention} sets out, a black rectangle is not a natural occlusion, the controls separate rule-relevant evidence from arbitrary image area rather than from other informative content, and they degenerate on the 13 largest targets. Sensitivity is an observable behavior, not faithful reasoning, and evidence is compared only at the level of rectangles. The benchmark measures neither interpretability, deployment reliability, nor injury reduction.

\textbf{Ethics and appropriate use.} A camera installed to check hard-hat compliance can equally support productivity monitoring, biometric identification, or retrospective review of a worker's conduct. Safety monitoring does not justify persistent surveillance; the two should be separated through retention limits, restrictions on secondary use, consent, and worker representation. The benchmark infers no identity, intent, or blame and should not be used for autonomous inspection or disciplinary monitoring: because its failure modes fall hardest on missed violations, deploying it without human review would shift risk onto workers while presenting an appearance of oversight. Appropriate use is research evaluation under documented scope limits and professional review, consistent with risk-management guidance for AI-enabled workplace tools \citep{nist2023airmf,howard2024workplace}.

\section{Conclusion}

ConstructionSafety-FaithBench separates answer correctness, evidence grounding, and evidence dependence rather than collapsing them into one score, and on an open-vocabulary grounding pipeline across four rule families the three came apart. Answer accuracy is therefore insufficient evidence of trustworthiness for safety-critical screening, and a tool's willingness to abstain deserves equal scrutiny: the dangerous failure is a confident verdict accompanied by unscored evidence. Every number reported here is regenerated by the released scripts, not transcribed by hand.
